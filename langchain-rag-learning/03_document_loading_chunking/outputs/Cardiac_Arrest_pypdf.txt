ABSTRACT 3 
A visual analysis may accurately predict cardiac arrest, making it a potent educational tool for raising 4 
public awareness of health issues. By predicting cardiac arrest earlier, preventative steps can be taken 5 
to save lives and the dissemination of such health knowledge can dramatically lower the world 6 
mortality rate. A heart attack, also known as cardiac arrest, encompasses various heart-related 7 
disorders and has been the leading cause of death worldwide in recent decades. Several medical data 8 
mining and machine learning technologies are being applied to gather helpful knowledge regarding 9 
heart disease prediction. The accuracy of the intended outcomes, however, is insufficient. This 10 
chapter aims to predict the likelihood of patients having heart disease to solve the issue. Specifically, 11 
it compared alternative models for the identification of cardiac arrest to appropriately categorize and 12 
forecast heart attack instances with compact features. The use of ensemble algorithms over classifier 13 
algorithms gives a maximum accuracy of 96.5%, which is examined in our investigation. 14 
Keywords: Cardiac Arrest, Machine Learning, Comparative Analysis, Heart Disease Prediction, 15 
Data Standardization, Supervised Learning, Health Education Awareness 16 
INTRODUCTION 17 
Visual analysis is an effective technique for educating society. Raising public awareness on various 18 
health issues can lead to taking preventative actions. For instance, through earlier cardiac arrest 19 
prediction, curative measures can be taken in advance to save human life (Maaliw, Alon, Lagman, 20 
Garcia, Susa, et al., 2022). Promoting such health knowledge in society can dramatically lower the 21 
world mortality rate. According to the World Health Organization (2021), cardiovascular diseases 22 
(CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality globally, killing an estimated 17.9 million people each 23 
year. Heart attacks and strokes are responsible for 85% of these fatalities. CVDs include coronary 24 
heart disease, cerebrovascular illness, rheumatic heart disease, and other diseases (Amini et al., 25 
2021). Nearly three-quarters of all heart-related fatalities occur in low- and middle-income nations. 26 
In 2015, low- and middle-income nations accounted for 82% of the 17 million early deaths (before 27 
the age of 70) related to non-communicable illnesses, accounting for a total of 37%. Four out of 28 
every five CVD fatalities are caused by heart attacks or strokes, among them, one-third of the deaths 29 
occur in those below 70-year old patients (Desai et al., 2021). High blood pressure, cholesterol, and 30 
lipid levels, as well as being overweight or obese, are all signs of heart illness (Jurgens et al., 2022). 31 
Identifying patients who are more likely to suffer from cardiac arrest and ensuring that they receive 32 
proper care would assist to prevent early deaths. All primary health care providers should give access 33 
to important noncommunicable disease drugs and basic health technologies to ensure that individuals 34 
in need receive treatment and counselling. Most heart attacks can be averted by addressing various 35 
behavioural risk factors such as cigarette use (Sandhu et al., 2012), poor nutrition and obesity (Garcia 36 
& Garcia, 2023), problematic alcohol consumption (Chudzińska et al., 2022), and lack of physical 37 
inactivity through population-wide initiatives. 38

SGD 488 
The term “stochastic” refers to a system or process that is subject to random chance. As a result, 489 
instead of selecting the entire data set for each iteration in Stochastic Gradient Descent, a few 490 
samples are chosen at random. The term “batch” is used in Gradient Descent to refer to the total 491 
number of samples from a dataset used to calculate the gradient for each iteration. The batch is taken 492 
to be the entire Dataset in traditional Gradient Descent optimization, such as Batch Gradient Descent. 493 
While using the whole Dataset is extremely useful for obtaining the minima with less noise, 494 
randomly, the problem arises when our datasets become large. This problem is solved using 495 
Stochastic Gradient Descent. SGD performs each iteration with a single sample, i.e., a batch size of 496 
one. The sample is randomly shuffled and chosen for iteration. 497
